Why the X-ray beam is polarized

The undulator makes each electron wiggle sideways. A charge that wiggles sideways radiates light whose electric field also points sideways. Relativity turns that light into a narrow beam of hard X-rays, and the beamline optics leave its direction alone. The beam reaches the sample with its electric field horizontal and across the beam, which is why the fluorescence detector belongs horizontal and at 90°.

The orange arrows are the beam's electric field. It swings back and forth horizontally, across the direction of travel, all the way from the undulator to the sample. The drawing is schematic. The real field oscillates about 3×1018 times per second with a wavelength near 1 Å.
24-ID-C after the upgrade: 6 GeV ring, 3.0 cm undulator, Si(111) mono, 12.662 keV.

1

Polarization is the direction the electric field swings

Light is a transverse wave. Its electric field E oscillates at right angles to the direction of travel. Polarization describes the path that the tip of E traces in that transverse plane.

If the field swings along one fixed line, the light is linearly polarized. If two perpendicular components oscillate out of step, the tip traces an ellipse, or a circle when the lag is 90°. The APS beam is linearly polarized along the horizontal.

Left: the wave travelling to the right, in perspective, over a green sheet that marks the horizontal plane. Right: looking straight down the beam. The dashed line is the path traced by the tip of E.
Equation 1.1The wave
\[ \mathbf{E}(z,t)=\hat{\mathbf{x}}\,E_{x}\cos(kz-\omega t)\;+\;\hat{\mathbf{y}}\,E_{y}\cos(kz-\omega t+\varphi) \]
\( z,\ t \)
Distance along the beam, and time.
\( \hat{\mathbf{x}},\ \hat{\mathbf{y}} \)
Unit vectors: horizontal across the beam, and vertical.
\( E_x,\ E_y \)
Amplitudes of the horizontal and vertical parts of the field.
\( k=2\pi/\lambda \)
Wavenumber. The field repeats every wavelength λ along the beam.
\( \omega=2\pi\nu=ck \)
Angular frequency. The field repeats ν times per second at a fixed point.
\( \varphi \)
How far the vertical part lags the horizontal part.

What it saysAny polarization is two perpendicular swings added together. With φ = 0 they stay in step and the tip moves along a line tilted by arctan(Ey/Ex). With φ = 90° and equal amplitudes the tip moves on a circle. For the APS beam Ey is nearly zero, so only the first term is left.

Equation 1.2Energy and wavelength
\[ E_{\text{photon}}=h\nu=\frac{hc}{\lambda}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad E\,[\text{keV}]=\frac{12.398}{\lambda\,[\text{\AA}]} \]
\( h \)
Planck's constant, 6.626×10−34 J·s, or 4.136×10−15 eV·s.
\( c \)
Speed of light, 2.998×108 m/s.
\( hc \)
Their product, 12.398 keV·Å. This is where the 12.398 comes from.
\( \nu,\ \lambda \)
Frequency and wavelength of the wave in equation 1.1.

What it saysPhoton energy and wavelength are the same information. Shorter wavelength means higher energy.

In numbershc = 4.136×10−15 eV·s × 2.998×108 m/s = 1.2398×10−6 eV·m = 12.398 keV·Å. At the Se K edge, 12.66 keV, λ = 12.398 / 12.66 = 0.979 Å and ν = c/λ = 3.06×1018 Hz.

Equation 1.3Degree of linear polarization
\[ P=\frac{I_{h}-I_{v}}{I_{h}+I_{v}} \]
\( I_h \)
Intensity carried by the horizontal field component, proportional to Ex².
\( I_v \)
Intensity carried by the vertical component, proportional to Ey².

What it saysP = 1 means all the intensity is horizontal. P = 0 means the two share it equally, as in unpolarized light. P = −1 means all vertical.

In numbersP = 0.98 means Ih = (1 + P)/2 = 99% of the intensity is horizontal and 1% is vertical.

An accelerating charge radiates along its acceleration

A charge that changes velocity sends a ripple out through the electromagnetic field. Two properties of that ripple matter for everything that follows. First, the radiated field points along the acceleration, as seen from where you stand. Second, if you look straight along the acceleration you see no radiation at all.

Equation 2.1The radiated field
\[ \mathbf{E}_{\text{rad}}=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^{2}R}\;\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{a})\;=\;-\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^{2}R}\;\mathbf{a}_{\perp} \]
\( q \)
The charge. For an electron q = −e.
\( \mathbf{a} \)

\( \hat{\mathbf{n}} \)
Unit vector from the charge to the observer: the line of sight.
\( R \)
Distance from charge to observer.
\( \mathbf{a}_{\perp} \)

\( \varepsilon_0 \)
Permittivity of free space, the constant that sets the strength of electric forces.

What it saysTake the acceleration, throw away the piece that points at you, and what is left gives the direction of the field you receive. Its size is a·sin ψ, where ψ is the angle between your line of sight and a, and it falls off as 1/R. This form holds for a slow charge. Step 4 handles the fast one.

Equation 2.2The doughnut, and Larmor's total
\[ \frac{dP}{d\Omega}=\frac{q^{2}a^{2}}{16\pi^{2}\varepsilon_0c^{3}}\,\sin^{2}\psi\qquad\qquad P_{\text{total}}=\frac{q^{2}a^{2}}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^{3}} \]
\( dP/d\Omega \)
Radiated power per unit solid angle in a given direction.
\( \psi \)
Angle between that direction and the acceleration.
\( P_{\text{total}} \)
The same pattern summed over all directions (the Larmor formula).

What it saysIntensity is field squared, so it goes as sin²ψ: greatest at right angles to the acceleration and zero along it. In three dimensions the pattern is a doughnut with its hole along a. Power also grows as a², so harder acceleration radiates much more.

3

The undulator makes the electrons wiggle sideways

An undulator is two rows of permanent magnets, one above and one below the electron beam, with poles that alternate every half period. Between the rows the magnetic field B is vertical, and it flips direction along the device.

A vertical field pushes a moving electron sideways. So the electron wiggles in the horizontal plane, and by step 2 a horizontal wiggle radiates horizontally polarized light.

Equation 3.1The magnetic field
\[ B_y(z)=B_0\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi z}{\lambda_u}\right) \]
\( B_y \)
The vertical magnetic field on the beam axis.
\( B_0 \)
Its peak value, set by the gap between the magnet rows. A smaller gap gives a larger B₀.
\( \lambda_u \)
The undulator period: the length of one N–S pair.

What it saysAlong the device the field swings smoothly between up and down, once per period.

Equation 3.2The sideways force
\[ \mathbf{F}=-e\,\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \gamma m\,\ddot{x}=e\,c\,B_0\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi z}{\lambda_u}\right) \]
\( -e,\ m \)
The electron's charge and rest mass.
\( \mathbf{v} \)
Its velocity, essentially c along the beam.
\( \gamma \)
The Lorentz factor, 11,742 at 6 GeV. It makes the electron γ times harder to push sideways.
\( \ddot{x} \)
Horizontal acceleration.

What it saysThe cross product of a forward velocity and a vertical field is horizontal. There is no vertical force at all, so the electron never leaves the horizontal plane. This one line is the origin of the beam's polarization.

Equation 3.3The path
\[ \frac{dx}{dz}=\frac{K}{\gamma}\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi z}{\lambda_u}\right)\qquad\qquad x(z)=-\frac{K\lambda_u}{2\pi\gamma}\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi z}{\lambda_u}\right) \]
\( dx/dz \)
The slope of the path: the angle between the electron's direction and the axis.
\( x(z) \)
The sideways position along the device.
\( K \)
The deflection parameter, defined in equation 3.4.

What it saysIntegrating the force once gives the slope and twice gives the position. The path is a sine wave with the same period as the magnets. Its largest angle is K/γ and its amplitude is Kλu/2πγ.

In numbersFor 24-ID-C after the upgrade: K = 1.85 and λu = 3.0 cm give a largest angle K/γ = 157 µrad and an amplitude of 0.75 µm.

Equation 3.4The deflection parameter
\[ K=\frac{e\,B_0\,\lambda_u}{2\pi\,m\,c}=0.934\;B_0[\text{T}]\;\lambda_u[\text{cm}] \]

What it saysK is a pure number that measures how hard the magnets steer. It is the largest steering angle in units of 1/γ, the natural width of the radiation cone (step 4). When K is about 1 or less, the electron's light always stays inside its own cone and the light from every period adds up in step. That is an undulator. When K is much larger, the cone sweeps side to side like a lighthouse. That is a wiggler.

In numbersK = 1.85 with λu = 3.0 cm needs B₀ = 1.85 / (0.934 × 3.0) = 0.66 T.

4

Relativity shrinks the wavelength and narrows the beam

A wiggle a few centimetres long should radiate radio waves a few centimetres long. It makes Ångström X-rays because the electron is travelling almost as fast as the light it emits. The electron nearly keeps up with its own light, so the crests from successive periods pile up close together.

Equation 4.1How fast is the electron?
\[ \gamma=\frac{E_e}{mc^{2}}\qquad\qquad \beta=\frac{v}{c}=\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}}\;\approx\;1-\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}} \]
\( E_e \)
The electron's total energy: 7 GeV in the APS before the upgrade, 6 GeV after it.
\( mc^2 \)
The electron's rest energy, 0.511 MeV.
\( \gamma \)
The Lorentz factor: total energy in units of rest energy.
\( \beta \)
Speed as a fraction of the speed of light.

What it saysThe electron falls short of light speed by only 1/2γ².

In numbersFor 24-ID-C after the upgrade the ring energy is 6 GeV, so γ = 6000 / 0.511 = 11,742. Then 1 − β = 1/2γ² = 3.6×10⁻⁹. Over one 3.0 cm period, light gains just 1.09 Å on the electron.

A race between the electron and its own light. Each time the electron starts a new magnet period it launches a new wave crest. Light is only slightly faster, so each crest is only slightly ahead of the next one. Push β toward 1 and the crests crowd together. The approximation 1/2γ² becomes accurate as β approaches 1.
Equation 4.2Slippage: the wavelength from a race
\[ \lambda\;=\;\underbrace{\frac{\lambda_u}{\beta}}_{\text{light travels}}-\underbrace{\lambda_u}_{\text{electron travels}}\;=\;\lambda_u\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}-1\right)\;\approx\;\frac{\lambda_u}{2\gamma^{2}} \]

What it saysThe electron needs a time λu/βc to cross one period. In that time light covers λu/β. The difference is how far one crest sits ahead of the next, and the spacing of crests is the wavelength. Putting in 1/β − 1 ≈ 1 − β ≈ 1/2γ² from equation 4.1 gives the result.

In numbers3.0 cm / (2 × 11,742²) = 1.09 Å, or 11.4 keV. This is the K = 0 limit. Equation 4.4 adds the corrections.

Equation 4.3The same answer by changing frames
\[ \lambda'=\frac{\lambda_u}{\gamma}\qquad\text{then}\qquad \lambda=\lambda'\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}}\;\approx\;\frac{\lambda'}{2\gamma}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \lambda\approx\frac{\lambda_u}{2\gamma^{2}} \]
\( \lambda' \)
The magnet period as the electron sees it. Riding with the electron, the undulator rushes past and is Lorentz-contracted by γ.
\( \sqrt{\tfrac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}} \)
The relativistic Doppler factor for a source coming straight at you. For β near 1 it equals 1/2γ.

What it saysIn its own frame the electron sits still while a field with a period of a few microns sweeps over it. It oscillates and radiates infrared light as a plain dipole. Seen from the lab, that dipole is approaching at nearly c, so its light is Doppler-compressed by another factor of 2γ. One γ comes from contraction and 2γ from Doppler.

In numbers3.0 cm / 11,742 = 2.55 µm in the electron's frame, then 2.55 µm / 23,484 = 1.09 Å in the lab.

Equation 4.4The undulator equation
\[ \lambda_n=\frac{\lambda_u}{2n\gamma^{2}}\left(1+\frac{K^{2}}{2}+\gamma^{2}\theta^{2}\right) \]
\( n \)
Harmonic number, 1, 3, 5 … on axis. The crests also line up if light slips ahead by n wavelengths per period.
\( K^2/2 \)
The wiggle correction. A wiggling path is longer than a straight one, so the electron's average forward speed drops to \( \bar\beta_z=1-\frac{1+K^{2}/2}{2\gamma^{2}} \). Light gains more per period and the wavelength grows.
\( \gamma^2\theta^2 \)
The off-axis correction. Seen at an angle θ from the axis, the light's head start is measured along a slanted line, which lengthens it: \( \lambda=\lambda_u(1/\bar\beta_z-\cos\theta) \).

What it saysThe on-axis wavelength is set by three knobs: ring energy, magnet period and K. The beamline tunes by changing the gap. Closing it raises B₀ and K, which lengthens λ and lowers the photon energy. Light that leaves at an angle is redder than light on the axis.

In numbersFor 24-ID-C after the upgrade, with about 70 periods, n = 3 and K = 1.85: θcen ≈ 85 µrad × √(2.70 / 210) ≈ 10 µrad, and Δλ/λ ≈ 0.5%.

5

Why it isn't quite 100%: off-axis light twists

On the axis the radiated field is exactly horizontal. Off the axis, the rule from step 2 still holds, but the acceleration with its line-of-sight part removed now has a small vertical piece. That piece is largest on the diagonals, which makes a four-leaf pattern of cross-polarized light.

A beamline accepts only a small solid angle around the axis, and the mono selects the on-axis energy, so most of the twisted light never reaches the sample. What arrives is typically 95–99% linearly polarized.

6

The mono and mirrors keep it, and can even purify it

A crystal reflects the two polarization components differently. The component perpendicular to the plane of the reflection is called σ, and it reflects fully. The component lying in that plane is called π, and it is weakened. Which component is which depends on the plane in which the mono turns the beam.

If the mono deflects vertically, the beam's horizontal field is σ and passes untouched, and the small vertical part is π and gets trimmed. If the mono deflects horizontally, the roles swap and the wanted horizontal field is the one that is weakened.

24-ID-C uses Si(111) with two reflections. 24-ID-E uses Si(220) at a fixed 12.662 keV. This guide assumes vertical deflection for 24-ID-C and a single horizontal reflection for 24-ID-E. Both are assumptions to check against the real optics, and the figure lets you switch them.

Equation 6.1Bragg's law
\[ \lambda=2d\sin\theta_B\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sin\theta_B=\frac{12.398}{2\;d[\text{\AA}]\;E[\text{keV}]} \]
\( d \)
Spacing of the reflecting atomic planes: 3.1356 Å for Si(111) and 1.9201 Å for Si(220).
\( \theta_B \)
The Bragg angle between the beam and those planes. The beam is turned through 2θB.

What it saysReflections from successive planes add in step only at one angle for each wavelength. Rotating the crystal selects the energy.

In numbersFor 24-ID-C after the upgrade: Si(111), d = 3.1356 Å. At 12.662 keV, sin θB = 12.398 / (2 × 3.1356 × 12.662) = 0.156, so θB = 8.98° and the beam turns through 18.0°.

Equation 6.2Channel-cut geometry
\[ w=\frac{g}{\tan\theta_B}\qquad\qquad h=2g\cos\theta_B \]
\( g \)
The gap between the two reflecting faces of the channel.
\( w \)
How far along the second face the beam lands, measured from the point directly across from the first footprint.
\( h \)
The height step between the outgoing beam and the incoming beam.

What it saysBoth faces are cut into one crystal, so they are always exactly parallel. Whatever angle satisfies Bragg's law on the first face satisfies it on the second, and the outgoing beam is always parallel to the incoming one. The price is that the faces cannot move relative to each other. When the crystal turns to a new energy, the beam lands at a different place on the second face, and the outgoing beam shifts in height a little.

In numbersOn Si(111) at 12.662 keV, θB = 8.98°: w = 6.3 gap widths and h = 1.98 gap widths. Between 6 and 20 keV the footprint walks from 2.9 to 10.1 gap widths along the second face, and the height step changes from 1.89 to 1.99 gap widths.

Equation 6.3The polarization factor of a reflection
\[ \frac{R_\pi}{R_\sigma}\approx C,\qquad C=\lvert\cos 2\theta_B\rvert \]
\( R_\sigma \)
Integrated reflectivity for the field perpendicular to the reflection plane.
\( R_\pi \)
Integrated reflectivity for the field lying in the reflection plane.

What it saysThis is step 2 again. The crystal's electrons are driven along the incoming field and must radiate into the reflected direction. For σ, the reflected direction is always at 90° to their motion, where a dipole radiates best. For π, the reflected direction is only 90° − 2θB away from their motion, so the field is reduced by sin(90° − 2θB) = cos 2θB. At 2θB = 90° a π reflection would vanish completely. The value C per bounce applies to a thick perfect crystal.

In numbersAt that angle C = cos 18.0° = 0.951. Two bounces give C² = 0.905.

Equation 6.4Polarization after the mono
\[ P_{\text{out}}^{\,\text{vertical}}=\frac{(1+P)-C^{m}(1-P)}{(1+P)+C^{m}(1-P)}\qquad\qquad P_{\text{out}}^{\,\text{horizontal}}=\frac{C^{m}(1+P)-(1-P)}{C^{m}(1+P)+(1-P)} \]
\( P \)
Polarization entering the mono. The horizontal share is (1 + P)/2 and the vertical share is (1 − P)/2.
\( m \)
The number of reflections: 2 for a channel cut or double-crystal mono, 1 for a single bounce.
\( C^m \)
The total π factor after m reflections.
vertical, horizontal
The plane in which the mono deflects the beam.

What it saysWhichever component is π is multiplied by C once per reflection, and then equation 1.3 is applied again. With vertical deflection the unwanted vertical light is π and is trimmed, so P rises slightly. With horizontal deflection the wanted horizontal light would be π and P would fall slightly.

In numbersP = 0.980 in gives 0.9819 out for 24-ID-C after the upgrade (2 reflections, vertical deflection). The other deflection plane would give 0.9779.

Equation 6.5Why the KB mirrors don't matter
\[ C_{\text{mirror}}=\lvert\cos 2\theta_g\rvert\approx 1-2\theta_g^{2} \]
\( \theta_g \)
The grazing angle of the beam on the mirror, a few milliradians.

What it saysThe same geometric factor applies to a mirror, but the beam is turned through such a small angle that σ and π are treated almost identically.

In numbersAt θg = 3 mrad, C = 1 − 2 × (0.003)² = 0.99998.

7

At the sample, the whole chain runs in reverse

The horizontal field drives every electron in the sample to oscillate horizontally, across the beam. Each one re-radiates as the dipole of step 2, with no emission along its line of oscillation. That line is the horizontal direction at 90° to the beam. A fluorescence detector placed there sees almost no scattered X-rays. Fluorescence has no memory of the beam's polarization, so it arrives equally in all directions.

The detector in this step is the fluorescence detector: the small energy-resolving SDD that sits beside the sample and is used for edge scans and element identification. It is not the large area detector downstream that records the diffraction pattern. That one stays on the beam axis and is drawn only for orientation.

Equation 7.1Thomson scattering by one electron
\[ \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=r_e^{2}\,\bigl|\hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}'\bigr|^{2}\;\;\longrightarrow\;\;r_e^{2}\Bigl[1-(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})^{2}\Bigr]=r_e^{2}\sin^{2}\psi \]
\( d\sigma/d\Omega \)
The differential cross-section: how strongly one electron scatters into a given direction.
\( r_e \)
The classical electron radius, \( e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0mc^2 \) = 2.818×10−15 m.
\( \hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\ \hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}' \)
Polarization directions of the incoming and the scattered X-ray.
\( \hat{\mathbf{n}} \)
Direction from the sample to the detector.
\( \psi \)
Angle between that direction and the incoming polarization.

What it saysThe beam's field shakes the electron along ε, and equation 2.2 gives what it radiates. The arrow marks the step of adding up both possible polarizations of the scattered X-ray, since the detector does not care which it receives. The result is the same sin²ψ doughnut, now with its hole along the beam's polarization.

Equation 7.2Where the detector is
\[ \sin^{2}\psi=1-\cos^{2}\!\phi_{\text{el}}\,\sin^{2}\!\beta_h\qquad\begin{cases}\phi_{\text{el}}=0: & \sin^{2}\psi=\cos^{2}\!\beta_h\\[2pt] \beta_h=90^{\circ}: & \sin^{2}\psi=\sin^{2}\!\phi_{\text{el}}\end{cases} \]
\( \beta_h \)
The detector's horizontal angle from the beam direction. In the horizontal plane this is the scattering angle 2θ.
\( \phi_{\text{el}} \)
The detector's elevation above the horizontal plane.

What it saysThis is equation 7.1 with the detector's position written out. Swinging the detector in the horizontal plane gives cos² of the angle from the beam, which is zero at 90°. Lifting it at 90° gives sin² of the elevation. Near the ideal spot both behave as the square of the offset, so the first few degrees cost little and the cost then climbs quickly.

In numbersA 10° offset in either direction gives sin²10° = 0.030. A 45° swing gives 0.5. Straight up gives 1.

Equation 7.3A beam that is not fully polarized
\[ \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=r_e^{2}\left[\,P\,\sin^{2}\psi\;+\;(1-P)\,\frac{1+\cos^{2}2\theta}{2}\,\right] \]
\( P \)
Degree of linear polarization from equation 1.3.
\( 2\theta \)
The scattering angle between the beam and the detector direction.

What it saysThe beam behaves as a fraction P of perfectly polarized light plus a fraction 1 − P of unpolarized light. The unpolarized part scatters with the familiar (1 + cos²2θ)/2 factor, which is ½ at 90° and never zero.

Equation 7.4The floor at the ideal spot
\[ \left.\frac{S}{S_{\max}}\right|_{\text{ideal}}\approx\;P\,\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\;+\;\frac{1-P}{2} \]
\( S/S_{\max} \)
Scatter into the detector, relative to the worst direction.
\( \alpha \)
Half-angle of the cone the sensor accepts, in radians.

What it saysEven at the ideal spot two things let scatter in. The sensor has a finite size, so most of it looks slightly off the axis, and sin²ψ averaged over a small disc is α²/2. And the unpolarized fraction scatters at half strength. A smaller sensor cone or a purer beam lowers one term each.

In numbersFor α = 9.1° = 0.159 rad and P = 0.98: 0.98 × 0.0126 + 0.010 = 0.022. The two terms are about equal. The pointing offset that doubles this floor is about 8.5°.

Equation 7.5Fluorescence does not care
\[ \frac{dN_{\text{fluor}}}{d\Omega}=\frac{N_{\text{fluor}}}{4\pi} \]

What it saysK-shell fluorescence is emitted uniformly over the full sphere of 4π steradians. The atom absorbs a photon, then emits one later with no memory of the direction or polarization of the first. Moving the detector at a fixed distance changes the scatter and leaves the fluorescence alone. The ratio of fluorescence to scatter is best where equation 7.2 is smallest.

Measure P on the beamline

Put a weak scatterer at the sample, such as water or a Kapton film. Count scattered X-rays in the fluorescence detector (SDD) at 90° horizontal, then at 90° vertical, at the same distance and in the same energy window. Subtract fluorescence and background first.

Equation 7.6Polarization from two counts
\[ I_{\text{hor}}\propto\frac{1-P}{2},\qquad I_{\text{vert}}\propto\frac{1+P}{2}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad P=\frac{I_{\text{vert}}-I_{\text{hor}}}{I_{\text{vert}}+I_{\text{hor}}} \]

What it saysPut ψ = 0 and 2θ = 90° into equation 7.3 and only the unpolarized half-term is left: that is the horizontal count. Put ψ = 90° and the polarized term adds in full: that is the vertical count. Their difference over their sum is P. A finite sensor cone leaks a little extra into the horizontal count (equation 7.4), so this slightly underestimates P unless you use a small aperture.

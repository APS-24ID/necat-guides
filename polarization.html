Why the X-ray beam is polarized

The undulator makes each electron wiggle sideways. A charge that wiggles sideways gives off light whose electric field also points sideways. Relativity squeezes that light into a narrow beam without turning the field, and the beamline optics leave it alone. So the beam reaches the sample with its electric field horizontal and across the beam. This guide follows that one property from the source to the sample.

Each section has a plain explanation and a figure you can adjust. The equations are folded away under the figures. Open any of them, or all of them with the button at the top, if you want the detail.

1

Polarization is the direction the electric field swings

Light is a wave of electric and magnetic field. The electric field E swings back and forth at right angles to the direction the light is traveling. Polarization is the name for the direction of that swing.

If the field swings along one fixed line, the light is linearly polarized. If it has two perpendicular swings that are out of step, the tip of the field traces an ellipse, or a circle. The beam at NE-CAT is linearly polarized, and the line is horizontal.

2

A wiggling charge radiates along its wiggle

Light is given off whenever an electric charge is shaken. Two facts about that light are all this guide needs.

First, the electric field of the light points along the direction the charge is being shaken, as seen from where you stand. Shake a charge left and right, and someone in front of it receives light whose field swings left and right.

Second, no light goes out along the line of the shaking itself. Someone standing exactly to the left or right of that charge sees nothing. The light goes out in the shape of a doughnut, with the hole along the direction of the shaking.

3

The undulator shakes the electrons horizontally

In the undulator the magnetic field points up and down. A vertical magnetic field pushes a moving electron sideways, never up or down. So the electron's wiggle lies entirely in the horizontal plane.

By step 2, a charge shaken horizontally gives off light whose field is horizontal. That is the whole origin of the beam's polarization. The guide How NE-CAT works has more on how the undulator works and how its energy is tuned.

4

Relativity squeezes the light forward but keeps its direction

The electron is moving at almost the speed of light, and that sweeps the doughnut of step 2 forward into a narrow cone. The squeeze acts only along the direction of travel. It does not turn the electric field. On the axis of the beam the field is still exactly horizontal.

The hole of the doughnut is still there too. It ends up as a dark ring around the beam in the horizontal plane, at an angle of 1/γ from the axis.

5

Why it isn't quite 100%: off-axis light twists

Exactly on the axis the field is exactly horizontal. A little way off the axis, the rule of step 2 still applies, but the geometry has changed: the direction of the shaking, seen from an angle, now has a small vertical part. That part is largest on the diagonals, and it gives a four-leaf pattern of light with the wrong polarization.

A beamline accepts only a small patch of light around the axis, and the mono selects the energy that belongs to the axis, so most of the twisted light never reaches the sample. What arrives is typically 95 to 99% linearly polarized.

For 24-ID-C after the upgrade: 1/γ = 85 µrad.

6

The mono and mirrors keep it, and can even purify it

A crystal treats the two polarization directions differently. The part of the field that sticks out of the plane in which the beam is turned is called σ, and it reflects fully. The part that lies in that plane is called π, and it is weakened. The reason is step 2 again: the crystal's electrons are being shaken along the field, and they cannot radiate well in a direction close to their own shaking.

So what the mono does to the polarization depends on which way it turns the beam. If it turns the beam up or down, the wanted horizontal field is σ and passes untouched, and the small unwanted vertical part is trimmed. If it turns the beam sideways, the wanted field is the one that is weakened.

24-ID-C uses Si(111) with two reflections. 24-ID-E uses Si(220) at a fixed 12.662 keV. This guide assumes that 24-ID-C turns the beam vertically and that 24-ID-E uses a single sideways reflection. Both are assumptions to check against the real optics, and the figure lets you switch them. The focusing mirrors turn the beam through such a small angle that they make no difference.

What it saysThis is step 2 again. The crystal's electrons are driven along the incoming field and must radiate into the reflected direction. The value C per bounce applies to a thick perfect crystal.

In numbersAt that angle C = cos 18.0° = 0.951. Two bounces give C² = 0.905.

7

What arrives at the sample, and how to check it

Set for 24-ID-C after the upgrade: P = 0.982 at the sample.

That number rests on an assumed 0.98 from the source and on the assumed mono geometry, so it is worth measuring. The polarization shows up directly in how the sample scatters: a polarized beam sends almost no scatter along its own field direction. Comparing the scatter seen from the side with the scatter seen from above gives the polarization.

The guide X-ray fluorescence at NE-CAT uses the same effect to decide where the fluorescence detector should sit.

Measure P on the beamline

Put a weak scatterer at the sample, such as water or a Kapton film. Count scattered X-rays in the fluorescence detector (SDD) at 90° horizontal, then at 90° vertical, at the same distance and in the same energy window. Subtract fluorescence and background first.

How NE-CAT works

NE-CAT runs two X-ray beamlines, 24-ID-C and 24-ID-E, at the Advanced Photon Source. Electrons circling the storage ring pass through rows of magnets and give off a narrow beam of X-rays. A silicon crystal picks out one energy, mirrors focus the beam to a few microns, and it lands on a protein crystal. This guide follows the X-rays from the ring to the detector.

Each section has a plain explanation and a figure you can adjust. The equations are folded away under the figures. Open any of them, or all of them with the button at the top, if you want the detail. Short on time? A walk down the beamline is the five-minute version, with no equations.

The path of the X-rays in five steps, not to scale. In the undulator, electrons wiggle between two rows of magnets and give off light, drawn as an orange wave. The monochromator reflects the light from two crystal faces and passes only one energy. The KB mirrors reflect it again and focus it. It hits the crystal at the sample, where most of it is caught by the beamstop, and the diffracted rays fan out to the detector. The sections below take these steps one at a time.

1

It starts with electrons in a ring

The APS storage ring is a 1.1 km circle of magnets that holds a beam of electrons moving at almost the speed of light. The electrons travel in bunches, and each bunch goes around about 270,000 times a second. The guide How APS works follows the electrons from the gun to the ring and explains how the ring holds them.

With the 24-ID-C after the upgrade settings, the ring holds 200 mA of electrons at 6 GeV. Each electron carries 11,742 times its rest energy.

The electrons give off X-rays wherever their path is bent. NE-CAT is at sector 24 of the ring. In the straight part of that sector the electrons pass through two undulators, one after the other. Each undulator makes X-rays while the electrons are inside it, and each feeds one beamline: the first 24-ID-C, the second 24-ID-E.

The APS Upgrade replaced the ring's magnets with a new arrangement. The old ring shut down in April 2023. The energy came down from 7 to 6 GeV and the current doubled from 100 to 200 mA. The biggest change is to the electron beam itself: it went from 280 µm wide to about 15 µm, and its electrons now travel far more nearly parallel. A smaller, straighter electron beam makes a smaller, brighter X-ray beam.

Left: the storage ring from above, with bunches of electrons circulating. The ring is divided into 40 sectors, and NE-CAT is at sector 24. Right: the straight section of sector 24, enlarged, with one bunch passing through. The electrons give off X-rays only while they are inside an undulator. The first undulator sends its X-rays to 24-ID-C. A small steering magnet then turns the electrons slightly, so the second undulator points in a slightly different direction and sends its X-rays to 24-ID-E. After that the ring's magnets bend the electrons away, and the X-rays carry straight on down the beamlines. The undulator makes the electrons wiggle

An undulator is two rows of permanent magnets, one above and one below the electron beam, with poles that alternate every half period. Between the rows the magnetic field points up, then down, then up again along the device.

A magnetic field pushes a moving charge sideways. So as an electron flies through, it is pushed left, then right, then left, and traces a gentle wiggle in the horizontal plane. Every time its path bends, it gives off light.

For 24-ID-C after the upgrade, each magnet period is 3.0 cm long. The wiggle is only 0.75 µm to either side.

The two rows can be moved toward or away from each other. The space between them is called the gap, and it sets how strong the field is on the beam. A smaller gap gives a stronger field and a bigger wiggle. In the figure, the gap is the first slider.

3

Why centimeter magnets make Ångström X-rays

A wiggle a few centimeters long should give off radio waves a few centimeters long. It gives off X-rays because the electron is traveling almost as fast as the light it emits.

Picture a race. Each time the electron starts a new magnet period it sends out a new wave crest. The light is only very slightly faster than the electron, so by the time the next crest is launched, the first one is only a tiny distance ahead. The crests end up packed close together, and closely packed crests are short-wavelength light.

For 24-ID-C after the upgrade, a 3.0 cm magnet period comes out as light of about 1.09 Å.

The beamline tunes the energy by opening and closing the gap between the magnet rows. A smaller gap means a stronger field and a bigger wiggle. A bigger wiggle slows the electron's forward progress a little, the crests spread out, and the photon energy drops.

4

Harmonics: one undulator, a ladder of energies

So far the undulator has made light of one wavelength. In fact it makes a ladder of energies at the same time. The lowest is called the fundamental, or first harmonic. Above it comes light at 3, 5, 7 … times that energy. These are the higher harmonics.

They come from the shape of the wave. When the field is weak the electron sways gently, and the electric field arriving on the axis is a smooth sine wave. A sine wave is a single energy. When the field is strong the electron swings through an angle wider than its own narrow cone of light. Someone on the axis is then lit only in a brief flash each time the electron points straight at them, which happens twice in every magnet period. The wave becomes a row of sharp spikes. A spiky wave is a smooth wave with faster ripples added at exact multiples of its rhythm, and those ripples are the harmonics.

Each flash points the opposite way to the one before, because the electron is swinging back. For that reason only the odd multiples survive on the axis. The even ones cancel there and show up only off to the side.

Closing the gap does two things. It slows the electron's forward progress, so every harmonic slides down in energy together. It also makes the wave spikier, which moves brightness out of the fundamental and into the higher harmonics. Opening the gap does the reverse. A beamline chooses the harmonic that is bright at the energy it wants.

For 24-ID-C after the upgrade, 12.662 keV is taken from harmonic 3 with K = 1.84.

All the harmonics travel down the beamline together. The monochromator of step 6 passes the one that is wanted. Light at a whole multiple of the chosen energy can also satisfy Bragg's law in the same crystal. The mirrors of step 7 reflect only below a cutoff energy, and that removes most of it.

Relativity also makes the beam narrow

The electron gives off light in all directions, as it sees things. Seen from the lab, the electron's speed sweeps nearly all of that light forward into a narrow cone, like the headlight of a fast train. The faster the electron, the narrower the cone.

An undulator narrows it further. Light from every one of its periods adds up in step only very close to the axis, so the bright center of the beam is much narrower than the cone from a single wiggle.

For 24-ID-C after the upgrade, the cone from one wiggle is about 85 µrad wide and the bright center about 10 µrad.

The electrons in the bunch are not all pointing in exactly the same direction, and that spread blurs the X-ray beam. This is where the upgrade shows most. In the old ring the horizontal spread was as large as the bright center of the beam. In the new ring it is several times smaller.

6

A silicon crystal picks one energy

The light from the undulator is bright but not a single energy. The monochromator, or mono, selects one. It is a perfect crystal of silicon. X-rays bounce off the layers of atoms inside it, and the reflections from all the layers add up only when the wavelength and the angle match. Turn the crystal and a different energy gets through.

24-ID-C uses a channel-cut crystal: a single block of silicon with a slot cut in it. The beam bounces off one wall of the slot and then the other, and leaves traveling in its original direction. Because both walls are part of one crystal they are always exactly parallel, which makes the mono very stable. 24-ID-C can be tuned across a wide range of energies. 24-ID-E uses a different cut of silicon and stays at one energy, 12.662 keV, which suits selenium.

For 24-ID-C after the upgrade: Si(111), set to a Bragg angle of 8.98° for 12.662 keV.

What it saysBoth faces are cut into one crystal, so they are always exactly parallel. Whatever angle satisfies Bragg's law on the first face satisfies it on the second, and the outgoing beam is always parallel to the incoming one. The price is that the faces cannot move relative to each other. When the crystal turns to a new energy, the beam lands at a different place on the second face, and the outgoing beam shifts in height a little.

In numbersOn Si(111) at 12.662 keV, θB = 8.98°: w = 6.3 gap widths and h = 1.98 gap widths. Between 6 and 20 keV the footprint walks from 2.9 to 10.1 gap widths along the second face, and the height step changes from 1.89 to 1.99 gap widths.

7

Mirrors focus the beam onto the crystal

X-rays pass straight through ordinary lenses and mirrors. They do reflect from a very smooth surface if they strike it at a very shallow angle, a fraction of a degree. A gently curved mirror used this way can focus them.

24-ID-C uses a pair of such mirrors, one behind the other. One focuses the beam in height and the other in width. This arrangement is called a Kirkpatrick-Baez pair, or KB mirrors. Together the mirrors form a small image of the X-ray source on the sample.

Published focus for 24-ID-C after the upgrade: 23 × 5 µm (h × v).

Apertures just before the sample can then trim the beam to match the size of the crystal.

The square shows the beam's cross-section at the sample. Choose a mirror and try each control. Curve moves the focus along the beam, so the spot grows in that mirror's direction only. Pitch changes the angle at which the beam strikes the mirror. It swings the reflected beam and is by far the most sensitive adjustment. Up / down (in / out for mirror 2) slides the mirror across the beam. The spot moves with it, and if the mirror goes too far part of the beam misses it or is blocked by its end. Roll twists the mirror about the beam and nudges the spot in the other mirror's direction. Yaw turns the mirror in its own plane and has almost no effect. The angles are drawn about forty times larger than life, and the effects of roll and yaw appear only in the square. Real mirrors meet the beam at a fraction of a degree and look flat to the eye. At such shallow angles even a magnified bend is too slight to see in the main drawing, so the curved bar under each mirror shows that mirror's bend on its own, magnified much further. The order drawn here, height first, is an assumption.

8

At the sample

The protein crystal is a fraction of a millimeter across. It sits in a small loop on the end of a pin, held at about 100 K in a stream of cold nitrogen gas. A goniometer holds the pin and rotates the crystal in the beam. 24-ID-C has an MD3-UP goniometer, which turns the crystal about a vertical axis. 24-ID-E has an MD2.

Most of the beam passes straight through the crystal and is caught by a small beamstop. A small part is diffracted by the ordered molecules in the crystal into thousands of separate rays. A large area detector behind the sample records where they land: an EIGER2 XE 16M on 24-ID-C and an EIGER 16M on 24-ID-E. As the crystal rotates, the detector collects the pattern of spots from which the structure is worked out.

A second, much smaller detector sits beside the sample. It measures the energy of each X-ray it receives, and is used to find which elements are in the crystal and to scan across an absorption edge. The beam's polarization decides where that detector should go. The guide Why the beam is polarized explains where the polarization comes from, and the guide X-ray fluorescence at NE-CAT explains how to use it.

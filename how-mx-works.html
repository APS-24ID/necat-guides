How MX works

Macromolecular crystallography, MX for short, finds where the atoms are in the large molecules of life: proteins, DNA and RNA, and machines as large as a ribosome or the shell of a virus. It does this by shining X-rays on a crystal of the molecule and working back from the pattern of spots the crystal throws onto a detector. This guide explains why the shape of a molecule is worth knowing, why the method needs a crystal, what the spots are, and how they are turned into a structure that can be trusted.

Each section has a plain explanation and a figure you can adjust. The equations are folded away under the figures. Open any of them, or all of them with the button at the top, if you want the detail. Short on time? How do we see a protein? is the five-minute version, with no equations.

The whole method in one line. A crystal turns in the X-ray beam, and the detector takes thousands of images of the spots it throws. From the spots a map of the electrons is worked out, atoms are fitted into the map, and the result is a model of the molecule, atom by atom. The protein here is myoglobin, drawn from its real structure. The images are a simulation of a small made-up crystal on the detector of 24-ID-C, and the map shows one helix of the protein.

1

Big molecules, and why their shapes matter

Macromolecules are the large molecules of life: proteins, DNA and RNA, and the assemblies they build together. They are large by the standards of chemistry. Myoglobin, a small protein, has about 1,260 atoms, not counting hydrogen. A ribosome has about 150,000, and the shell of a virus several hundred thousand. Crystallography can find the positions of nearly all of them. Hydrogen atoms are usually too faint to see, and parts of a molecule that do not hold still in the crystal do not show up.

A protein is a chain of amino acids, folded into a particular shape. The chain can be short or long: insulin has 51 amino acids, a typical protein has a few hundred, and the largest have thousands. The shape is a large part of what lets a protein do its job, but not the whole of it. The chemistry of the amino acids matters as well, and so does the way the protein moves. An enzyme has a pocket that fits one molecule and no other, lined with the chemical groups that carry out its reaction. An antibody has a surface that matches its target in shape and in charge. Many proteins change shape as they work. A structure shows all of this at once: which atoms are where, what they are next to, and, from structures caught in different states, how they move.

Knowing where the atoms are turns a name in a genome into a mechanism. It shows how an enzyme speeds up its reaction and why a single changed amino acid causes a disease. It shows where a drug binds and what could be changed to make it bind better. Many of the medicines in use today were designed with the structure of their target in hand.

The Protein Data Bank, the public archive of these structures, passed 250,000 entries in March 2026. Most of them were determined by X-ray crystallography.

Why X-rays, and why there is no X-ray microscope

Large as these molecules are, their atoms are no bigger than any others. Bonded atoms are about 1.5 Å apart: fifteen billionths of a centimeter. Light cannot show detail much finer than half its wavelength, and the wavelength of visible light is several thousand ångströms. Seeing atoms takes light with a wavelength of about one ångström, and that is an X-ray.

There is a second difficulty. A microscope uses a lens to gather the light scattered by an object and bring it back together as an image. No lens can do that for X-rays finely enough to show atoms. In crystallography the scattered X-rays are recorded as they are, and the work of the lens is done afterward by calculation.

3

Why a crystal: one molecule is far too faint

X-rays are scattered by electrons, and a single protein molecule does not have many. The scatter from one molecule is far too weak to measure, and the X-rays needed to try would destroy it first.

A crystal solves both problems. It is the same molecule repeated in the same orientation: about a hundred billion times, for a typical crystal. The repeat runs in all three dimensions: rows side by side make a layer, and layers stack to make the crystal. Each copy scatters the same faint wave. In most directions those waves are out of step and cancel. In certain directions they are exactly in step, and add. The scatter of the whole crystal is gathered into those directions as sharp, bright spots.

The crystal also shares out the damage. Every X-ray that is absorbed damages a molecule somewhere, but the pattern is the average over all the copies, and it survives until a good fraction of them have been hit.

The price is that the molecule has to be persuaded to crystallize, which is often the hardest step of all. A guide to how crystals are grown will be added to this series. A protein crystal is a fragile thing, about half water, held together by a few weak contacts between neighbors. That water is also why the structure in a crystal is close to the one the molecule has in the cell.

4

Where the spots fall, and why the crystal is turned

The positions of the spots say nothing about the molecule. They are set by the lattice: the size and shape of the unit cell, the block that repeats to make the crystal. A large unit cell gives spots that are close together, and a small one gives spots that are far apart.

Each spot can be thought of as a reflection of the beam from one set of parallel planes through the lattice. A set of planes reflects only when the beam meets it at exactly the right angle: the angle at which the waves reflected from one plane and the next are exactly in step. That condition is Bragg's law, named for William Lawrence Bragg, who worked it out in 1912. It ties the angle of a spot to the spacing of its planes and the wavelength of the X-rays, and it is given with the equations below. With the crystal held still, very few sets of planes are at their angle, and only a few spots appear.

So the crystal is turned in the beam while the detector records. The figure below shows why, using a construction called the Ewald sphere. As it turns, one set of planes after another comes to its angle, flashes, and passes on. A full data set is a few hundred to a few thousand images covering a half turn or more, and can hold hundreds of thousands of spots.

How far the crystal must turn depends on its symmetry. The unit cell often holds several copies of the molecule, each a turned or shifted copy of the others. Proteins are built from amino acids of one handedness, so their crystals cannot contain mirror images, and that leaves 65 ways of arranging the copies, called space groups. Symmetry in the crystal makes sets of spots equal in brightness, so a crystal of high symmetry gives a complete data set from a smaller turn.

The angle of a spot from the direct beam says how fine a detail it reports on. Spots near the middle of the detector come from widely spaced planes and carry the overall shape of the molecule. Spots near the edge come from closely spaced planes and carry the fine detail. The resolution of a data set is the spacing of the finest planes that still give measurable spots. The guide How NE-CAT works shows how the beam energy and the detector distance set what the detector can catch, and How does an X-ray detector work? shows how the detector counts the X-rays in each spot.

5

What a spot is: one wave of electron density

The positions of the spots come from the lattice. Their brightness comes from the molecule, and this is where the structure is.

What X-rays see is electron density: how many electrons there are at each point in the unit cell. Picture it as a landscape, with a peak at every atom. Because the crystal repeats, the landscape repeats, and a repeating pattern has a property that Joseph Fourier found in the early 1800s. It can be built exactly by adding up simple waves, and the only waves needed are those that fit the repeat a whole number of times: one crest across the unit cell, then two, then three, and so on. A sum of this kind is called a Fourier series. Its broad waves give the general shape of the landscape, and its finer and finer waves give the detail.

Each spot in the diffraction pattern is one wave of that series. The position of the spot says which wave it is: the direction its crests run in, and how many of them cross the unit cell. Those counts are the three index numbers of the spot, written h, k and l. The brightness of the spot says how tall the wave is: the height is the square root of the brightness. In effect, the crystal takes its own electron density apart into waves and sends each one to a different place on the detector.

To add a wave to the sum, one more thing is needed: where its crests lie within the unit cell. That is the wave's phase. Shift a wave by half its spacing and it puts a valley where there was a peak. The height and the phase of a wave together are called the structure factor of its spot.

Add up all the waves, each with its height and its phase, and the electron density appears. This sum is called a Fourier synthesis, and it is the calculation that does the work of the missing lens. Leave out the fine waves, and the result is a blurred version of the density. That is what resolution means: a 3 Å data set has no waves finer than 3 Å in it.

The catch: the phases are not recorded

The detector records how bright each spot is. That gives the height of each wave. It does not record the phase, and as the figure above shows, the phases matter more than the heights. Half of what is needed to build the density is missing.

This is the phase problem, and every structure determination has to get around it. There are several ways to do so, and each is a subject of its own. Later guides will take them up. For the rest of this guide, take it that the phases have been found.

7

The map, and the model built into it

With heights and phases for all the spots, the waves can be added up. The result is the electron density map: a three-dimensional picture of where the electrons are in the unit cell. It is drawn as a surface, like a net, around the regions where the density is high.

The map is not yet a structure. A structure is a list of atoms and their positions, and someone, or some program, has to work out which atoms account for the density. The amino acid sequence is known, so the task is to thread a known chain through the map.

A macromolecule has thousands of atoms to place, so the figure below looks at one short piece: thirteen amino acids of a helix of myoglobin. How hard the job is depends on the resolution. Near 1 Å, individual atoms can be seen. By 1.5 Å they have begun to merge, though rings still show the holes in their middles. At 2 Å the shapes of the side chains are clear, and ordered water molecules show up. At 3 Å the chain can be traced and the larger side chains placed. At 4 Å helices appear as rods, and the model rests heavily on what is already known about how proteins are built.

8

Refinement: making the model fit, and knowing when to stop

A first model is roughly right. Refinement makes it better. From the model, the brightness of every spot can be calculated and compared with what was measured. The atoms are then moved, a little at a time, to make the two agree, while bond lengths and angles are kept close to their known values.

Each round improves the phases too, because the phases now come from a better model. The new map shows things the old one did not: a side chain in a different position, a bound water, a ligand. The model is corrected, and the cycle repeats.

A special map guides the corrections. The difference map shows where the data call for more density than the model has, and where they call for less. In the first figure below it is drawn in green and red over a model with a mistake in it. An atom in the wrong place sits in red, where the data call for less, and leaves green where it belongs. The second figure shows the same thing for four atoms on a line, and lets the model be refined.

The danger is fitting the model to the noise. The check is to set aside a few percent of the spots at the very start and never use them in refinement. If the model is truly improving, it will predict those spots better too. That score is called R-free, and it is the first number to look at in judging a structure, along with the resolution.

Refinement stops when further changes no longer lower R-free and the difference map is flat. The model is then checked against what is known of chemistry, apart from the data: its bond lengths and angles, the twists of its backbone, and whether any atoms sit too close together. Last, the structure is deposited in the Protein Data Bank together with the measured spots, so that anyone can recalculate the map and judge the model for themselves.

9

What limits a structure

The crystal. No measurement can show more than the crystal holds. If the molecules are not all in quite the same position and orientation, the fine waves blur out, and the spots at the edge of the detector fade into the background. Much of the work of crystallography is in finding conditions that give better-ordered crystals.

Radiation damage. The X-rays that give the pattern also destroy the crystal. Each absorbed photon sets loose electrons that break bonds. Crystals are held in a stream of nitrogen gas at 100 K, which slows the spread of the damage many times over, but does not stop it. The fine detail goes first. The guide Why are we freezing our crystals? explains why the cold helps.

Many crystals. When no one crystal can give a full data set, the data are gathered from many: a few images from each of hundreds or thousands of small crystals, combined afterward. This is serial crystallography. It spreads the dose so thinly that data can be collected at room temperature, where the molecule moves as it does in life, and with short enough exposures it can follow a reaction as it happens.

The beam. A crystal can take only so much dose, however it is delivered. What a brighter and smaller beam offers is the chance to use smaller crystals, to pick out the best part of a crystal, and to collect quickly. The guides How APS works and How NE-CAT works describe where that beam comes from.

Crystallography is not the only route to a structure. Nuclear magnetic resonance, NMR, gives the structures of small proteins in solution, with no crystal at all. Electron microscopy of frozen single particles needs no crystal and does well with large assemblies. Computer prediction gives a good first model for many proteins. Crystallography remains the most direct route to fine detail, and to seeing exactly how a drug, a substrate or a water molecule sits in its site. It is also fast: once crystals are in hand, a data set takes minutes or less.

X-ray fluorescence at NE-CAT

A protein crystal is nearly all carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and hydrogen, and none of those shows up in the fluorescence detector. What does show up is the handful of heavier atoms: the selenium grown into the protein to solve its structure, a metal the protein holds, or a heavy atom soaked in. Each gives off X-rays at energies of its own once the beam is above its absorption edge. This guide explains where that fluorescence comes from, which atoms give it, what it is used for at NE-CAT, and where the detector has to sit to see it clearly.

Each section has a plain explanation and a figure you can adjust. The equations are folded away under the figures. Open any of them, or all of them with the button at the top, if you want the detail.

Looking down on a protein crystal in the beam. The gray dots stand for the carbon, nitrogen and oxygen that make up nearly all of it, and the green dots are selenium atoms. Most X-rays in the beam pass straight through. Now and then one is absorbed by a selenium atom, which flashes and sends out an X-ray of its own, in a direction that has nothing to do with the beam's. The few that happen to head for the fluorescence detector are counted. They pile up at selenium's own two energies, below the energy of the beam. 1

An atom absorbs one X-ray and gives back another

The electrons in an atom sit in shells. The two in the innermost shell, the K shell, are held the most tightly. An X-ray can knock one of them out of the atom, but only if it carries more energy than it takes to free that electron. That threshold is the atom's K absorption edge, and every element has its own.

Losing the electron leaves a hole in the K shell. In about a millionth of a billionth of a second an electron from a shell further out drops in to fill it. The energy it gives up is the difference between the two shells, and that difference is fixed for each element.

The atom gets rid of that energy in one of two ways. It can send it out as an X-ray: this is fluorescence. Or it can hand it to another of its electrons, which leaves the atom instead. That one is called an Auger electron, and the detector never sees it. Light atoms mostly take the second route, and heavy atoms mostly the first.

The fluorescence X-ray always has less energy than the X-ray that started it, and its energy does not depend on the beam's. An electron dropping in from the L shell gives the line called Kα. One from the M shell gives Kβ, a little higher in energy and several times weaker. Heavy atoms such as platinum and mercury do the same thing one shell further out. Within the energy range of the beamline it is their L shell that is emptied, and the lines are called Lα and Lβ.

The atom keeps no memory of where the first X-ray came from, so fluorescence goes out equally in all directions.

What it saysThe first condition is the absorption edge. Below it the X-ray cannot free a K electron, and no fluorescence follows. The other two are bookkeeping: the X-ray given off carries the difference between the shell the electron leaves and the shell it lands in. Both lines lie below the edge, which is why fluorescence can always be told apart from the beam that caused it.

In numbersFor selenium the K edge is at 12.658 keV. The L and M shells are held by about 1.45 and 0.16 keV, so Kα is at 11.21 keV and Kβ at 12.50 keV. Each shell is really a few levels some tens of electron-volts apart, which is why tables list Kα1 and Kα2 separately.

2

Which atoms in a protein crystal fluoresce

A protein crystal is mostly carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and hydrogen, in the protein and in the water that fills about half of it. These atoms do absorb the beam now and then, but they almost never answer with an X-ray, and the few X-rays they do give off have so little energy that the sample and the air stop them. The fluorescence detector does not see them at all.

Sulfur, in methionine and cysteine, and phosphorus, in DNA and RNA, do a little better. Their lines are near 2 keV. Most of that is lost on the way out of the sample and through the air, and what arrives is at the very bottom of the detector's range. Atom for atom, sulfur sends the detector less than a thousandth of what selenium does. A 50 kDa protein has fifteen or twenty sulfur atoms, so all of its sulfur together amounts to about a hundredth of one selenium atom.

What the detector does see is the handful of heavier atoms.

Selenium. Selenomethionine is methionine with its sulfur replaced by selenium. A protein grown with it carries one selenium atom for roughly every 5 to 10 kDa. It is by far the most common label for phasing, and so the most common reason to measure fluorescence.

Metals the protein holds. Iron in hemes and iron–sulfur clusters, zinc, copper, manganese, nickel, cobalt and molybdenum. There may be only one such atom in a large protein.

Heavy atoms added on purpose. Mercury, platinum, gold, lead, osmium, the lanthanides, bromide and others, soaked into the crystal for phasing.

Atoms that came along with the solution. Zinc, calcium, bromide, arsenic from cacodylate buffer, or nickel left over from purification.

Fluorescence says that an element is in the beam. It does not say where the atoms are, or whether they sit in the same place in every copy of the protein. A metal in the liquid around the crystal fluoresces just as well as one bound in an active site.

3

Edges and lines within reach at 24-ID

Both the edge and the lines move up steadily with atomic number, so each element has its own place on the energy scale. For an element to fluoresce, the beam energy has to be above its edge. For an edge scan, the beamline also has to be able to step its energy across that edge.

24-ID-C can be set anywhere in 6–20 keV. That takes in the K edges of the metals from manganese to zinc, and of arsenic, selenium and bromine. It also takes in the L edges of the lanthanides and of the heavy atoms used for soaks, such as platinum, gold and mercury.

Elements whose edges lie below that range still fluoresce when they are in the beam. Calcium, iodine and xenon are common examples. Their lines can be seen, but their edges cannot be scanned here.

The line is always well below the edge: 1.45 keV below for selenium. That gap is what lets the detector separate the fluorescence from the beam's own scattered X-rays, which come later in this guide.

4

The edge scan: finding the energies for phasing

The most common use of fluorescence at a crystallography beamline is the edge scan. The beam energy is stepped across the absorption edge of one element, about an electron-volt at a time. At each step the detector counts the X-rays in a narrow window around that element's line. Below the edge there is nothing to count. As the beam crosses the edge, the fluorescence switches on.

The fluorescence at each energy is in proportion to how strongly the element absorbs there. That absorption is one of the two numbers that describe how an atom's scattering changes near its edge. It is called f″. The other, f′, is how much the atom's ordinary scattering is reduced, and it can be worked out from the whole f″ curve. A program such as CHOOCH does this in a moment once the scan ends.

The scan is needed because the edge is not in exactly the same place, or the same shape, in every sample. The chemistry around the atom shifts it by a few electron-volts and often puts a sharp peak, called the white line, at the top of the rise. A table can say where the edge of the bare element is. Only a scan says where it is in this crystal.

Two energies come out of the scan. The peak is where f″ is largest. It gives the largest differences between Friedel pairs, and it is the energy for a SAD experiment. The inflection, partway up the rise, is where f′ is most negative. A MAD experiment adds data there, and at a remote energy well above the edge, to get the differences between energies as well.

Edge scans are run on 24-ID-C, where the energy can be changed. 24-ID-E stays at 12.662 keV, just above the selenium edge, so selenium data can be collected there without one.

5

Finding out which metal is there

The second use needs no scan. Set the beam well above the edges of interest and record the whole spectrum: counts against the energy of the X-rays arriving. Each element in the beam puts a pair of peaks at its own energies, and a table of line energies names it.

This is the way to check that the selenium went in, to find out which metal a protein picked up when it was made, to tell zinc from nickel or copper in a binding site, or to confirm that a soak worked before spending beam time on a data set.

Some care is needed in reading a spectrum. The Kβ line of one element lies close to the Kα line of the next one up: iron Kβ and cobalt Kα are only 0.13 keV apart, which is about as close as the detector can separate. An element whose edge is above the beam energy stays silent, so the beam has to be set above every edge that matters. And the detector records whatever the beam and its scatter touch. Steel and brass parts near the sample can add iron, chromium, nickel, copper and zinc lines of their own.

6

What else reaches the detector: scatter

Fluorescence is not the only thing that comes off the sample. Every electron in it also scatters the beam, and there are far more electrons than atoms of the element being looked for.

Elastic scatter. An X-ray bounces off the electrons in the sample and keeps all its energy. It shows up at the energy of the beam.

Compton scatter. An X-ray bounces off an electron and knocks it away, losing a little energy as it does. It shows up just below the energy of the beam.

The fluorescence detector is a small SDD beside the sample, which measures the energy of every X-ray it receives. It can tell scatter from fluorescence by energy. What it cannot do is count without limit. When too many X-rays arrive, the beam has to be turned down, and from then on every scatter count takes the place of a fluorescence count.

One selenium atom just above its edge gives off as much Kα as the scatter from a large amount of protein and water.

7

A polarized beam leaves one direction dark

The beam's electric field is horizontal and across the beam. It shakes every electron in the sample along that line. A shaken electron gives off light in the shape of a doughnut, with none at all along the line of the shaking. So there is one direction that receives almost no scatter: horizontal, at 90° to the beam. A fluorescence detector placed there still receives its full share of fluorescence. The guide Why the beam is polarized explains why the beam is polarized this way.

Real atoms add a dependence on angle of their own. In elastic scatter the electrons of an atom scatter in step with each other at small angles and fall out of step at large ones. So elastic scatter is very strong toward the beamstop and weak at 90° and behind. Compton scatter is the other way around. An electron can only be knocked free by a hard enough push, and a small-angle scatter is a gentle one, so Compton scatter is weak near the forward direction and strong at 90° and behind. At the ideal spot most of the little scatter that remains is Compton.

Together these mean that moving the detector toward the beamstop is worse than moving it the same amount toward the source.

What it saysAt small angles all the electrons in an atom scatter together, so the intensity goes as the square of their number. At large angles their waves fall out of step and mostly cancel. It leaves out interference between neighboring molecules and the sharp Bragg spots from the crystal.

In numbersAt 12.66 keV and 90°, x = 0.72 Å−1. Oxygen's form factor has fallen from 8 to about 1.6 there, so its elastic scatter is down about 25 times from the forward direction.

What it saysCompton scatter obeys the same polarization factor as elastic scatter, so the ideal spot suppresses it too. Its dependence on angle is the reverse of elastic scatter. A bound electron cannot recoil from a gentle push, so there is little Compton scatter near the forward direction and a lot at 90° and behind. For light atoms at 90° it is the larger of the two.

In numbersFor water at 12.66 keV, seen from the ideal spot with a 9° sensor cone: about 74% of the scatter is Compton and 26% is elastic.

8

How close? The sensor sees a cone, not a line

The sensor is a small disc, not a point. Seen from the sample it covers a cone of directions. Only the middle of that cone looks straight along the dark direction. Its edges look a few degrees to one side, where there is some scatter.

Moving the detector closer widens the cone. The fluorescence collected goes up with the area of the cone: at half the distance, four times as much. The scatter goes up faster, because a wider cone reaches further into the bright lobes. There is also a limit to how clean the mix can get. The beam is not perfectly polarized, and the unpolarized part scatters in every direction. Once the cone is narrow enough for that part to dominate, backing away loses fluorescence and gains nothing.

Which end to favor depends on the sample. More fluorescence collected means less beam, and so less radiation damage, for the same counts. A cleaner mix matters when the fluorescence is weak. The design study for the new detector on 24-ID-C settled on about 15 mm from the sample to the sensor, behind an opening 2.4 mm in radius. That gives a cone of 9° half-angle, which the other figures in this guide use. These are design values, not measurements of the installed detector.

What scatter costs: a noisier edge scan

Scatter does not land in the window an edge scan counts, because its energy is too high. What it does is fill the detector. The beam is turned down until the detector is counting at a rate it can handle, and if most of what it counts is scatter, most of that rate is wasted. The fluorescence counts are then few, and the scan is noisy.

How much this matters depends on how strong the fluorescence is to begin with. A selenomethionine crystal gives so much that the detector can be badly placed and the scan still looks fine. With one metal atom in a large complex, or a small crystal in a large drop of liquid, the placement can decide whether the edge is seen at all.

If the beam is so weak that the detector never reaches its working rate, scatter costs almost nothing. The figure shows the usual case at an undulator beamline, where the beam is strong and has to be turned down.

10

Rules of thumb

What will fluoresce. Any element heavier than about calcium that is in the beam, once the beam energy is above its edge. The light atoms of the protein itself will not.

Which energy. For an edge scan, the element's edge has to lie within the range of the beamline. For a spectrum, set the beam above every edge that matters, and remember that each line sits well below its own edge.

Where the detector goes. In the horizontal plane at 90° to the beam. The first few degrees away from that cost little, and after that the cost climbs quickly. Staying within about 5° keeps the scatter under one and a half times its minimum, and within about 8° keeps it under twice. If the detector cannot go there, moving it toward the source costs the least, lifting it costs slightly more, and moving it toward the beamstop costs the most.

How close. Closer collects more fluorescence, and farther collects a cleaner mix. For a sensor opening of 2.4 mm radius, about 15 mm is a reasonable middle. Go closer for the lowest dose, and farther back when the fluorescence is weak.

What this guide leaves out. The sample is treated as water for scatter, with every atom scattering on its own. A real sample also sends sharp reflections and diffuse scatter toward the area detector, and the loop, the pin and any ice add scatter of their own. The air along the beam path scatters too, and the detector sees some of that path unless it is shielded. Fluorescence that has to cross a thick sample is absorbed more than the figures here allow for, and so is the beam on its way in. None of this moves the ideal spot. It does change the totals.
